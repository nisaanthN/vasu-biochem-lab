## The core formula

Every standard amino acid has the same core: an **α-carbon** bonded to (1) an amino group, (2) a carboxyl group, (3) a hydrogen, and (4) a **side chain (R group)**. The R group is the source of all variation — proline is the lone exception (its amino group is part of a ring, making it an imino acid).

At physiological pH the amino group is protonated (-NH₃⁺) and the carboxyl is deprotonated (-COO⁻). The molecule carries both a positive and negative charge — a **zwitterion**.

## Classification by R group polarity

The single classification you must drill is by side-chain chemistry.

### 1. Non-polar, aliphatic (7)
Glycine (G), Alanine (A), Valine (V), Leucine (L), Isoleucine (I), Methionine (M), Proline (P). Bury inside protein cores driven by hydrophobic effect.

### 2. Aromatic (3)
Phenylalanine (F), Tyrosine (Y), Tryptophan (W). Absorb UV at 280 nm — the basis of protein quantification by spectrophotometry.

### 3. Polar, uncharged (5)
Serine (S), Threonine (T), Cysteine (C), Asparagine (N), Glutamine (Q). Hydroxyls and amides — H-bond donors and acceptors.

### 4. Acidic (negatively charged at pH 7) (2)
Aspartate (D), Glutamate (E). Side-chain carboxyl.

### 5. Basic (positively charged at pH 7) (3)
Lysine (K), Arginine (R), Histidine (H). Histidine's pKa is ~6.0, so it's the *only* amino acid that meaningfully buffers at physiological pH — that's why it sits in enzyme active sites doing proton shuttling.

<Mnemonic phrase="Essential AAs: PVT TIM HALL — Phe, Val, Thr, Trp, Ile, Met, His, Arg, Leu, Lys">
Indian pharmacy textbooks list these 9 (or 10 with arginine, which is conditionally essential) as nutritionally essential — must come from diet. Histidine and arginine are sometimes called "semi-essential" because they're essential in infants but not stable adults.
</Mnemonic>

## Zwitterions and isoelectric point

At low pH (acidic), all ionisable groups are protonated: -NH₃⁺ and -COOH. Net charge **+1**.
At high pH (basic), all are deprotonated: -NH₂ and -COO⁻. Net charge **−1**.
Somewhere between these, the molecule has zero net charge — its **isoelectric point (pI)**.

For an amino acid with only the α-amino and α-carboxyl groups (most non-polar and polar uncharged AAs):

```
pI = (pKa(COOH) + pKa(NH₃⁺)) / 2
```

For glycine specifically: pKa(COOH) = 2.34, pKa(NH₃⁺) = 9.60, so pI = 5.97.

For amino acids with an ionisable side chain:

- **Acidic** (Asp, Glu): pI = average of the two **lowest** pKa values (the COOH and the side-chain COOH).
- **Basic** (Lys, Arg, His): pI = average of the two **highest** pKa values (the NH₃⁺ and the side-chain basic group).

### Worked example: aspartate

pKa₁ (α-COOH) = 1.88; pKa₂ (β-COOH side chain) = 3.65; pKa₃ (NH₃⁺) = 9.60.

Aspartate is acidic. Average the two lowest:
pI = (1.88 + 3.65) / 2 = **2.77**.

At pH 2.77, aspartate has equal positive (NH₃⁺) and negative (one COO⁻) charges — net zero.

<Callout variant="info" title="Pharmacy connection: isoelectric precipitation">
Insulin's pI is ~5.4. Industrial purification of insulin precipitates it at pH 5.4 where its solubility is minimum (no net charge → no electrostatic repulsion between molecules → aggregation). This is the same logic behind casein precipitation in cheese-making and the recrystallisation step in many protein-drug manufacturing processes.
</Callout>

## Titration curve and net charge

A titration curve for an amino acid has **inflection points at each pKa**. Net charge at any pH can be computed by summing the fractional protonation of every ionisable group:

```
Fraction protonated = 1 / (1 + 10^(pH − pKa))
```

For glycine at pH 7: COOH is essentially fully deprotonated (pH ≫ pKa1), NH₃⁺ is essentially fully protonated (pH ≪ pKa2). Net charge ≈ 0 (one each, cancelling).
