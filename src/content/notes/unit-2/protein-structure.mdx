## Why the hierarchy matters

A protein's three-dimensional shape *is* its function. A 1 °C shift in body temperature is enough to kill some enzymes; a single amino-acid swap can turn haemoglobin into sickle haemoglobin. For a pharmacy student this hierarchy explains why heat sterilisation works, why some drugs are formulated lyophilised, and why a "biosimilar" requires more validation than a generic small molecule.

## Primary structure — the sequence

Primary structure is the **linear amino-acid sequence**, conventionally written from the N-terminus (free α-amino group) to the C-terminus (free α-carboxyl group).

The only bond holding it together is the **peptide bond** — a partial-double-bond amide formed by condensation between one AA's carboxyl and the next AA's amino. Three structural consequences flow from the partial double bond:

1. **Planarity**: the six atoms of the peptide bond lie in a plane.
2. **Rigidity**: rotation around the C–N bond is restricted.
3. **Trans preference**: cis peptide bonds are strained (proline is the exception).

```
   O    H
   ||   |
   C — N
  / \   \
H₃N    R   ← rotation allowed at Cα-N and Cα-C (φ and ψ angles)
```

<KeyPoint>
Primary structure encodes everything. The sequence determines folding (the Anfinsen experiment), which determines function. A single point mutation can abolish activity (e.g., HbS → sickle-cell disease) or alter drug metabolism (CYP2D6 polymorphisms in poor metabolisers).
</KeyPoint>

## Secondary structure — local patterns

Local repeating geometries stabilised by **backbone hydrogen bonds** (between the C=O of one residue and the N-H of another). The R groups don't participate.

### α-helix

- A right-handed coil with 3.6 residues per turn.
- H-bonds form between residue *i* and residue *i+4*.
- Rises ~5.4 Å per turn, ~1.5 Å per residue.
- Side chains point outward.

**Helix breakers**: proline (rigid ring, no NH for H-bond) and glycine (too flexible).

### β-sheet

- Extended strands H-bonded side-by-side.
- **Parallel** (N → C on both strands going the same way) or **antiparallel** (opposite). Antiparallel is slightly more stable.
- Side chains alternate above and below the sheet plane.
- Common in fibrous proteins (silk fibroin) and the cores of globular proteins.

### β-turn and Ω-loops

Short reversals (4 residues for β-turn) that let the polypeptide fold back on itself. Often contain proline (cis-peptide) and glycine (flexibility) precisely because they're geometrically constrained.

<Mnemonic phrase="α-helix = αcademic (intramolecular, within a single strand). β-sheet = βetween (between strands).">
Frame it physically: an α-helix's hydrogen bonds run *within* one polypeptide. A β-sheet's hydrogen bonds run *between* separate strands of the polypeptide (or between separate chains entirely).
</Mnemonic>

## Tertiary structure — the full 3D fold

The overall 3D shape of a single polypeptide. Stabilised by **side-chain interactions**:

| Interaction | Type | Strength | Where |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hydrophobic effect | Entropic | Strong (cumulative) | Non-polar Rs bury in core |
| Hydrogen bonds | Electrostatic | Moderate | Polar Rs, often with water |
| Ionic (salt bridges) | Electrostatic | Moderate | Asp/Glu ↔ Lys/Arg |
| Disulphide bonds | Covalent | Strong | Between two cysteines (forms cystine) |
| Van der Waals | Dispersion | Weak (cumulative) | Throughout the core |

The **hydrophobic effect** is the dominant force. Burying non-polar side chains releases ordered water molecules — a large entropy gain that overwhelms the loss of conformational entropy in folding.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why disulphides are a pharmacy concern">
Insulin has two disulphide bonds. Reducing agents (DTT, β-mercaptoethanol, even high concentrations of vitamin C) reduce them, unfolding insulin and destroying activity. This is why insulin formulations are protected from light and oxidisers and stored cold.
</Callout>

## Quaternary structure — multimers

Quaternary structure is the assembly of **two or more polypeptide chains** into a functional protein. The same forces that stabilise tertiary structure (hydrophobic, ionic, H-bonds, disulphides) stabilise the subunit interfaces.

### Examples relevant to pharmacy

- **Haemoglobin**: 2α + 2β subunits, each carrying a haem group. Allosteric — binding O₂ at one subunit shifts the others toward higher affinity (cooperativity, sigmoidal binding curve).
- **Insulin**: stored as a Zn²⁺-stabilised hexamer; active form is the monomer. Long-acting insulins (glargine, detemir) deliberately tune the hexamer/monomer equilibrium to delay absorption from the injection site.
- **IgG antibody**: 2 heavy + 2 light chains held by disulphides — the basis of every monoclonal antibody drug.
- **DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase**: many-subunit machines.

## Denaturation — losing structure

Denaturation = loss of higher-order structure (secondary, tertiary, quaternary) **with primary structure intact**. Common denaturing conditions:

| Agent | What it disrupts |
| :--- | :--- |
| Heat | All non-covalent interactions; eventually peptide bond too |
| Strong acid / alkali | Ionic interactions, H-bonds; can hydrolyse peptide bond |
| Urea, guanidine HCl | H-bonds (by competing); 8 M urea fully unfolds most proteins |
| Detergents (SDS) | Hydrophobic core (replaces with detergent micelle) |
| Reducing agents (DTT, β-ME) | Disulphide bonds specifically |
| Heavy metals (Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺) | Bind to -SH groups, disrupt disulphides |
| Mechanical shear | Hydrogen bonds, ionic |

Renaturation is sometimes possible (Anfinsen's classic ribonuclease experiment) but usually irreversible at scale — folding requires chaperones in the cell. This is why food cooking, autoclaving, and many sterilisation methods rely on denaturation.
