## The Michaelis–Menten model

The single most testable topic in BP203T. Two pharmacologists, Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten, modelled enzyme kinetics in 1913. Their model assumes:

1. The enzyme **E** and substrate **S** form a transient **ES complex**.
2. ES breaks down to **E + P** (product), with rate constant `k_cat`.
3. The reverse step (P → ES) is negligible (initial-rate conditions).
4. **[ES] is in steady state** (its rate of formation equals its rate of breakdown).

This gives the classic equation:

```
       Vmax · [S]
v  =  ────────────
       Km + [S]
```

Where:

- **Vmax** = maximum rate when every active site is filled (saturated with substrate).
- **Km (Michaelis constant)** = the substrate concentration at which v = Vmax/2.

## What Km and Vmax tell you

<KeyPoint>
**Km is an inverse measure of affinity.** Low Km = enzyme is half-saturated at low [S] = binds substrate tightly. High Km = needs lots of substrate = binds weakly.
</KeyPoint>

- Vmax depends on **enzyme concentration** (`Vmax = k_cat · [E]_total`) and the catalytic rate constant.
- Km depends only on the **enzyme–substrate identity**, not on [E].
- **k_cat / Km** is the *specificity constant* — the diffusion-limited upper bound is ~10⁸ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ (a "kinetically perfect" enzyme).

### Numbers worth remembering

| Enzyme | Km (mM) | k_cat (s⁻¹) | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hexokinase (glucose) | 0.1 | 1000 | Low Km — keeps phosphorylating even at low glucose |
| Glucokinase (liver, glucose) | 10 | 100 | High Km — only active at high blood glucose, e.g., after a meal |
| Catalase (H₂O₂) | 25 | 4 × 10⁷ | Kinetically near-perfect |
| Acetylcholinesterase (ACh) | 0.09 | 1.4 × 10⁴ | Why neostigmine prolongs ACh action so effectively |

The hexokinase / glucokinase comparison is a classic exam pair — same reaction, different Km, different physiological role.

## The shape — a rectangular hyperbola

At low [S], the relationship is roughly linear (`v ≈ Vmax · [S] / Km` when [S] ≪ Km).
At high [S], v plateaus at Vmax (when [S] ≫ Km).

Exactly at [S] = Km, v = Vmax / 2. That's how you read Km off the v vs [S] plot.

## Lineweaver–Burk — the double reciprocal plot

Take the reciprocal of both sides:

```
1     Km    1      1
─  = ──── · ─  +  ────
v    Vmax  [S]   Vmax
```

This is `y = mx + b` with:

- **y-intercept** = 1 / Vmax
- **x-intercept** = -1 / Km
- **slope** = Km / Vmax

So the LB plot turns the hyperbola into a straight line. The three inhibition modes change the slope or intercept in distinctive ways — which is exactly why we use it.

## The three inhibition modes

### Competitive inhibition

- Inhibitor resembles substrate and binds the **active site**.
- Adding more S out-competes the inhibitor.
- **Vmax unchanged** (you can still reach it with enough S).
- **Km appears larger** (you need more S to reach Vmax/2).

LB plot: same y-intercept (1/Vmax), different x-intercept (more negative; smaller magnitude).

**Example**: methotrexate competing with folate for dihydrofolate reductase. Statins competing with HMG-CoA for HMG-CoA reductase.

### Non-competitive inhibition

- Inhibitor binds an **allosteric site** (somewhere other than the active site), available whether substrate is bound or not.
- Adding more S **cannot rescue** activity.
- **Vmax decreases** (some enzyme is permanently locked out).
- **Km unchanged** (the active site still works the same — there's just less of it).

LB plot: same x-intercept (-1/Km), different y-intercept (higher; lower Vmax).

**Example**: heavy metals binding -SH groups on cysteines distal to the active site. Some allosteric drugs.

### Uncompetitive inhibition

- Inhibitor binds **only the ES complex**, not free E.
- Adding more S actually *helps* the inhibitor bind more (since there's more ES).
- **Both Km and Vmax decrease** by the same factor.

LB plot: parallel lines (same slope) shifted up.

**Example**: lithium on inositol monophosphatase (relevant to bipolar pharmacology).

### Summary table

| Mode | Km | Vmax | LB intercepts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Competitive | ↑ | unchanged | Same y-int, more neg x-int |
| Non-competitive | unchanged | ↓ | Same x-int, higher y-int |
| Uncompetitive | ↓ | ↓ | Parallel lines (same slope) |

<Mnemonic phrase="Competitive Catches the substrate site. Non-competitive Negates the enzyme. Uncompetitive only Unmasks itself when ES exists.">
Three Cs and an exception. Each term cues which site the inhibitor binds — which then determines what happens to Km and Vmax.
</Mnemonic>

## Calculating apparent Km and Vmax

For competitive inhibition with inhibitor concentration `[I]` and `Ki`:

```
Km_app = Km · (1 + [I] / Ki)
Vmax_app = Vmax
```

For non-competitive:

```
Km_app = Km
Vmax_app = Vmax / (1 + [I] / Ki)
```

For uncompetitive:

```
Km_app = Km / (1 + [I] / Ki)
Vmax_app = Vmax / (1 + [I] / Ki)
```

## Allosteric enzymes — when Michaelis-Menten breaks down

Multi-subunit enzymes with cooperative binding don't follow MM kinetics. Their v vs [S] curve is **sigmoidal**, not hyperbolic. Examples: aspartate transcarbamoylase, phosphofructokinase-1, haemoglobin (binding O₂, not technically an enzyme but the canonical example).

The Hill equation models cooperativity:

```
            [S]^n
v  = Vmax · ─────────
           K + [S]^n
```

where `n` is the Hill coefficient (n > 1 indicates positive cooperativity).
