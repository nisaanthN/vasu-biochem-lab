## What glycolysis does

Glycolysis converts one molecule of **glucose (6C)** into two molecules of **pyruvate (3C)** in the cytoplasm. It happens in every cell that uses glucose — aerobically or anaerobically — and is the only ATP source for red blood cells (no mitochondria) and the only major source for skeletal muscle during burst exercise. The net yield is modest: 2 ATP, 2 NADH per glucose.

But the pathway matters far beyond the ATP. Its intermediates feed into the pentose phosphate pathway, gluconeogenesis (reverse), glycogen synthesis, and serine/glycine synthesis. Many anti-cancer and anti-parasitic targets sit in this pathway.

## The 10 steps — two phases

**Phase 1 — Energy investment (steps 1–5)**: spend 2 ATP to phosphorylate and prepare the sugar.
**Phase 2 — Energy payoff (steps 6–10)**: produce 4 ATP and 2 NADH from the two triose pieces.

### Step-by-step

```
1.  Glucose                  ──Hexokinase / Glucokinase──►  G6P            (uses ATP)  IRR
2.  G6P                      ──Phosphohexose isomerase──►   F6P            (reversible)
3.  F6P                      ──PFK-1──►                     F1,6BP         (uses ATP)  IRR
4.  F1,6BP                   ──Aldolase──►                  DHAP + G3P     (reversible)
5.  DHAP                     ──Triose-P isomerase──►        G3P            (reversible)
6.  G3P                      ──G3P dehydrogenase──►         1,3-BPG        (makes NADH)
7.  1,3-BPG                  ──Phosphoglycerate kinase──►   3PG            (makes ATP, SLP)
8.  3PG                      ──Phosphoglycerate mutase──►   2PG            (reversible)
9.  2PG                      ──Enolase──►                   PEP            (loses water)
10. PEP                      ──Pyruvate kinase──►           Pyruvate       (makes ATP, SLP)  IRR
```

Three irreversible steps (1, 3, 10) — these are the **regulatory chokepoints**.

## The three regulatory steps in detail

### Step 1: Hexokinase / Glucokinase

Glucose + ATP → Glucose-6-phosphate + ADP.

- **Hexokinase** (all tissues): low Km (~0.1 mM), product-inhibited by G6P. Active even at low glucose, but saturates fast.
- **Glucokinase / Hexokinase IV** (liver, pancreatic β-cells): high Km (~10 mM), not product-inhibited. Only kicks in when blood glucose rises after a meal.

This Km difference is why hepatocytes "wait" to take up glucose until blood levels rise — letting other tissues feed first when glucose is scarce.

### Step 3: Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) — the rate-limiting step

F6P + ATP → F1,6BP + ADP.

PFK-1 is the **most heavily regulated enzyme** in glycolysis:

| Activator | Sensor of |
| :--- | :--- |
| AMP, ADP | Low energy charge — need ATP |
| F2,6BP | Insulin (postprandial state — burn glucose) |
| Pi | Energy depletion |

| Inhibitor | Sensor of |
| :--- | :--- |
| ATP | High energy — don't make more |
| Citrate | TCA already saturated |
| H⁺ (low pH) | Lactic acid accumulating |

F2,6BP is the most potent allosteric activator and is itself made by PFK-2 (a separate enzyme regulated by insulin/glucagon — the metabolic switch between fed and fasted state).

### Step 10: Pyruvate kinase

PEP + ADP → Pyruvate + ATP.

- Activated by F1,6BP (feed-forward activation).
- Inhibited by ATP, alanine (a deamination product of pyruvate — signals enough pyruvate around).
- Liver isoform is also inactivated by glucagon-stimulated phosphorylation (fasted state — don't waste glucose).

**Pyruvate kinase deficiency** is the second-most-common hereditary RBC enzymopathy (after G6PD deficiency) — causes haemolytic anaemia.

## Substrate-level phosphorylation

Glycolysis makes ATP without an electron-transport chain. Two SLP steps:

- **Step 7** (PGK): 1,3-BPG transfers its high-energy phosphate to ADP.
- **Step 10** (PK): PEP transfers its phosphate to ADP.

Each generates 1 ATP per triose, so 2 per glucose. Combined with the 2 ATP investment at steps 1 and 3, **net = 2 ATP per glucose**.

## NAD⁺ regeneration — the anaerobic problem

Step 6 reduces NAD⁺ to NADH. NAD⁺ is a limited resource. If it's not regenerated, glycolysis stops. Two options:

- **Aerobic** (with O₂): NADH is shuttled into mitochondria (via malate-aspartate or glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle), reoxidised by the ETC. Pyruvate goes on to acetyl-CoA and the TCA cycle.
- **Anaerobic** (no O₂): pyruvate is reduced to **lactate** by lactate dehydrogenase, regenerating NAD⁺. This is how exercising muscle, RBCs, and the Cori cycle in liver work.

<KeyPoint>
Lactic acidosis isn't a sign that the muscle is making "bad" lactate — it's a sign that glycolysis is running flat-out without enough O₂ for the ETC. Lactate is the metabolic exhaust of fast-twitch metabolism.
</KeyPoint>

## Pharmacology and clinical correlations

- **Arsenate (AsO₄³⁻)**: a phosphate analogue that uncouples step 7 — 1,3-BPG forms but the high-energy bond goes to arsenate instead of ATP. Net result: pathway still runs, but no ATP from glycolysis.
- **Iodoacetate**: covalently inactivates GAPDH (step 6) by alkylating its active-site cysteine.
- **Fluoride**: inhibits enolase (step 9) — relevant to fluoride additive in blood-glucose collection tubes (prevents glycolysis ex vivo and stabilises the glucose reading).
- **2-Deoxyglucose**: an investigational antitumor agent. Phosphorylated to 2DG-6-P which can't proceed in glycolysis, depleting ATP in glycolysis-dependent tumor cells.

## Galactose and fructose entry

- **Galactose** → galactose-1-P → glucose-1-P → glucose-6-P (enters at step 2). Defects: **galactosemia** (galactose-1-P uridyltransferase deficiency).
- **Fructose** → fructose-1-P → glyceraldehyde + DHAP (enters mid-pathway, bypassing PFK-1 regulation). This bypass is why excess fructose intake drives lipogenesis more readily than glucose — there's no PFK-1 brake.

## The Cori cycle

Skeletal muscle exports lactate to liver. Liver converts lactate back to glucose via **gluconeogenesis** (essentially reverse glycolysis with workarounds at the three irreversible steps). Glucose re-enters circulation and goes back to muscle. Net: muscle gets ATP; liver pays the metabolic cost (gluconeogenesis is ATP-expensive — 6 ATP equivalents per glucose).
